---
title: The Gift of a Moment
description: Ichigo ichie, osmanthus, and old photos — many of the moving things in this world are, by nature, gifts of a moment
date: 2026-10-19
type: note
duration: 3min
---

While sorting through old photos, I realized the earliest one dates back to 2018. I flipped through them one by one; each held a stretch of old time, and I couldn't quite tell whether the feeling was sweet or bitter — just something in between. By the end, there were many I still couldn't bring myself to delete.

I seem to have been born with this instinct: when I meet something beautiful, my first reaction is to want to keep it. As if by keeping it, that stretch of time wouldn't drift away.

But these past weeks the whole city has smelled of osmanthus, and it keeps reminding me: many of the moving things in this world are brief by nature. The osmanthus doesn't bloom longer for anyone, and its scent doesn't linger for anyone — it simply arrives on time, and fades on time.

In Japanese tea ceremony there is a phrase, *ichigo ichie* — one time, one meeting. It says: the encounter you are having right now happens only once in a lifetime; it cannot be copied, and it cannot be repeated. And precisely because it cannot be repeated, you should give yourself to it completely. So the solemnity with which a tea master lifts the bowl is not ritual for its own sake — it is clarity: the clear knowledge that this bowl of tea, once drunk, is drunk.

Su Shi wrote of the clear breeze on the river and the bright moon over the mountains, calling them free for all and inexhaustible. But read closely, and the possession he speaks of was never ownership — it is that the ear receives it as sound, and the eye meets it as color. The moment the wind reaches your ear, the moment the moonlight enters your eyes — only then does it truly belong to you. What we can truly own is never the thing itself, but our perception of it at that moment.

Once I understood this, looking again at those photos I couldn't delete, something in me loosened by an inch.

There is a saying: if the end of something leaves you especially sad, its process must have been especially beautiful. Read in reverse, it turns into a comfort — that I could have a stretch of time I am this reluctant to part with is itself fate slipping me candy. It is precisely because the process was brilliant enough that the emptiness at the end aches a little. Like finishing a good show: the moment the credits roll, you sit there stunned, and it takes days to come back. But that hollow feeling is itself proof of how good it was.

Think a little deeper: often when we fear commitment, when we fear taking things seriously, what we are actually dodging is not the effort itself but the drop after the joy. But if, to avoid the emptiness after the curtain falls, you won't even show up for the opening — that is the real miss. Remember: pain is not merely the price of brilliance. Pain is the echo brilliance leaves behind. One who has never been full cannot speak of emptiness.

So rather than practicing indifference, practice saying goodbye well. Life holds so many brief beauties like this: catch the instant of their arrival completely; look carefully, feel fully; and when they leave, allow yourself the wistfulness, then bid them a gentle farewell.

We are always searching for beauty that never fades, yet many of the moving things in this world are, by nature, gifts of a moment.

As for those photos, I will probably keep them always. Only now, when I look through them again, the frame of mind is different — no longer a refusal to let go, but one small reunion after another. The osmanthus will bloom again next year; but this year's city full of fragrance happens only once in a lifetime.
